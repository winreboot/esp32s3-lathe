Help and Wiring Guide

Work safely. Unplug the lathe and turn off the G540 supply before touching any wiring. The lathe's speed-pot terminals may be at mains potential, so treat them as live until you have proven otherwise. This system never replaces the E-stop and never drives the steppers.

What this does

Three Omron encoders watch the lathe. A 600 P/R encoder on each slide measures its real position for the DRO, and the 100 P/R spindle encoder measures speed and direction. The ESP32 shows all of it here and on a color display at the machine, records jobs and controls spindle speed. UCCNC still moves the axes.

X and Z position
600 P/R encoders, through level shifter module LS2 (12 V on the cable, 3.3 V at the ESP32). Counted in hardware at 2400 counts per revolution
Spindle speed and direction
100 P/R encoder. It also feeds the G540, so UCCNC can cut threads, and the ESP32 reads it through level shifter module LS1
Spindle control
Through a PC817 optocoupler that replaces the lathe's speed pot, with no electrical connection to the lathe controller
At the machine
2.8 in color display with the DRO, RPM, target, loaded tool, alarms and status, plus two status LEDs

The five rules that protect your hardware

  1. 3.3 V only on ESP32 pins. Its pins are not 5 V tolerant. That is why every encoder and G540 line goes through a level shifter whose LV side is fed from the 3V3 pin, and never straight to a GPIO.
  2. Use E6B2-CWZ6C encoders only (NPN open collector). Voltage-output versions such as the E6B2-CWZ3E would put their supply voltage on the ESP32 pins.
  3. Never join ESP32 ground to the lathe controller. The PC817 is the only link between them.
  4. Never power the encoders from G540 terminal 11. That supply can reach 50 V and the encoders' limit is 24 V.
  5. The lathe's pot voltage must be under 25 V. That is the rating of the 10 µF capacitor across it (the PC817's output itself takes 35 V). Measure it before connecting.

What the display shows
Three full-width rows sized to read from across the shop: X, Z, then spindle RPM. The digits are about 13 mm tall on a 2.8-inch screen, half as tall again as before. Beside the RPM are the target, a speed bar with the target marked, and the loaded insert; an active alarm replaces the insert. Wi-Fi and CNC status are on the web page and the LEDs.
Tap the screen (anywhere; the three short lines in the X row's corner are the reminder) to open the Settings menu: Zero X, Zero Z, X shows diameter or radius, screen normal or upside down, Colours (swap red and blue, and a dark blue or black background), calibrate touch, View (what fills the screen: everything, RPM only, X only, Z only, or X and Z; choose RPM only until the axis encoders are fitted, and the two axes stop showing zero), Info (firmware version, Wi-Fi address and signal, the .local address, spindle run hours and powered-on hours), Close, and Reboot, which asks you to confirm. It closes on its own after 30 seconds. The very first tap runs the touch calibration instead: press the three crosses as they appear, and the menu opens. At power-up the screen shows a drawn lathe with the firmware version for two seconds. If the colours look wrong (the workpiece in that picture should be brass yellow, and in the menu the squares marked R, G and B should be red, green and blue), open Colours and turn on Swap red / blue. The same screen switches the Background between dark blue and black. The spindle hours count every second the spindle turns, are saved every minute, and are the same figure as the Run hours card on this page. Everything set here is saved; the diameter and upside-down switches are also in the web page's Settings.

Between the cables and the ESP32 sit three ready-made 4-channel bidirectional level shifter modules (the small BSS138 boards sold for I2C, with HV, LV, GND and four channel pins on each side) and one PC817 optocoupler module. Nothing is soldered: the modules come with header pins, so the wiring is jumper wires plus a terminal strip or lever connectors for the shared points. The milled interface board on the next tab does the same job and remains an alternative.

Why a shifter, and why 12 V on its HV side

The encoders have open-collector outputs: they can pull a line to 0 V but never drive it high, so every line needs a pull-up. The shifter module has 10 kΩ pull-ups built in, on both sides. With the encoder supply (12 V) on the HV side, the cables carry a 0–12 V swing that shrugs off noise, and the spindle lines sit at the same 12 V that the G540's own inputs pull them to, so the two never fight. The LV side, fed from the ESP32's 3V3 pin, delivers clean 3.3 V to the GPIOs. The module is bidirectional and needs no direction setting. The charge-pump line is different: it is a 5 V output from the UC100, so its module (LS3) gets 5 V on the HV side, not 12. Use a 12 V encoder supply, not 24 V, so the HV side matches the G540.

Spindle control: the PC817 stage stays

A shifter has no isolation, and the lathe's speed-pot circuit may sit at mains potential, so the optocoupler is not optional. Use a ready-made 1-channel PC817 module with a 3.3–5 V input (the LED resistor is on the board) whose output terminals are the bare transistor, marked C and E (or OUT+ and OUT−). Avoid the type with a VCC / OUT / GND output: it needs a supply on the lathe side.

The 4.7 kΩ and the 10 µF are the only loose parts left; join them with the WIPER and LOW wires in a small terminal strip or on a scrap of perfboard. Everything on the output side belongs to the lathe: keep it inside the controller enclosure, insulated, and never let it touch ESP32 ground. Mount the PC817 module so the gap between its input and output pins stays clear and dry; on some modules that gap is only 6–8 mm, and it is all the creepage there is.

Alternative to the level shifter modules. The interface board sits between the cables and the ESP32. Every cable lands on a screw terminal (J1–J9), so anything can be unplugged. It is a two-sided board, 144 × 94 mm, designed to be milled on a desktop CNC from a 150 × 100 mm double-sided blank, with no plated holes. The picture shows the board from the component side: the top copper and its two traces, the bottom copper shown through the board, and every cable in its real color.

Reading the board
  • Left edge: the encoders and the optional DB25 tap: J3 spindle encoder, J6 DB25 tap, J1 X encoder, J2 Z encoder. Each encoder's shield goes in the 0V/SH screw together with the blue wire.
  • Top edge: J4 12 V supply in the corner, then J5, four wires to the G540 (terminals 2, 3, 1 and 12).
  • Right edge: J7 and J8 to the ESP32. Pin names are printed beside each screw.
  • Top right corner: U1 and J9, the isolated lathe side, behind a slot cut through the board.
  • Bottom copper: every signal trace. Ground is all the copper left after the traces are isolated, and it reaches every GND and 0V/SH screw on its own.
  • Top copper: two traces, orange for +12V and red for 3V3, which cross over the bottom traces (they replace the old wire links). The rest of the top is ground copper. Every hole that is not GND sits in its own small copper-free circle, so the isolation cut leaves one clean groove around each lead and no lead can touch the top ground. GND holes have no ring, because they are meant to touch it.
  • Gold rings: via pins V1–V15. The holes are not plated, so each via is a bare wire soldered on both sides. V1–V7 join the top traces to the bottom; V8–V15 tie the top ground to the bottom ground.
  • Lathe corner: neither side has any copper within 8 mm of the lathe side along the board surface, and nothing closer than 6 mm in a straight line through the 2 mm slot under U1.
  • Align pins: two 3 mm holes on the centre line, used to line the board up when you flip it. One is near the bottom edge and one above the middle, so the board only goes onto the pins one way round: it cannot be fitted upside down by mistake.

Both mill files have the same layers, and each layer is one machining operation, so nothing has to be interpreted: ISO_CENTER (the cutter's centre lines for a 0.5 mm cutter: follow them with no offset), CLEAR_POCKET (closed areas to pocket out; they run 1 mm past the board edge), COPPER_REF (the copper that stays, with its holes drawn too: for checking only, never cut it), DRILL_0P8 and DRILL_1P3 (holes at true size, one layer per drill), ALIGN_3P0 (the two alignment holes), SLOT (the 2 mm isolation slot, a closed shape to pocket) and OUTLINE (the board edge, a plain rectangle). NOTES is text. Units are millimetres (the file says so in its header), and the origin is the board's bottom-left corner.

Milling the board

  1. Set up. Use a fresh, flat 150 × 100 mm double-sided blank; don't strip either side. Stick it to a flat spoilboard with double-sided tape, and probe or surface the area if your machine can. The board is 144 × 94 mm, centred with 3 mm spare all round. The DXF origin is the board corner, so set your work zero 3 mm in from the blank's corner, and note it: you keep the same X and Y zero for both sides.
  2. Side 1: alignment holes. Load the …_bottom.dxf file and drill the two ALIGN_3P0 holes, 3 mm, right through the blank and about 5 mm into the spoilboard. They sit on the board's vertical centre line, the line you flip the board around, one near the bottom edge and one above the middle.
  3. Side 1: copper. Engrave along the ISO_CENTER lines, with no offset (an "on the line", engrave or follow-path operation), about 0.1 mm deep, with a cutter 0.5 mm wide or less at that depth (a 30° V-bit, or a 0.3–0.5 mm end mill). The lines are already the centre line for a 0.5 mm cutter, so a 0.5 mm cutter leaves exactly the copper drawn in COPPER_REF; a narrower one leaves every pad and trace a little larger, plus a thin strip in the middle of some gaps, which the pockets take out. Every copper shape has its own closed line and no two lines come closer than the cutter width, so your CAM should show no merged loops, bubbles or bone shapes; if it does, it is offsetting the lines or the cutter is set wider than 0.5 mm. Then pocket the CLEAR_POCKET areas 0.1–0.15 mm deep with a 0.8–1.6 mm end mill (any size: the CAM offsets for its own tool), to take the copper off completely. Do not cut COPPER_REF; it is only there to compare against.
  4. Side 1: drill. Drill the DRILL_0P8 circles at 0.8 mm (resistors, transistors, U1, capacitors and vias) and the DRILL_1P3 circles at 1.3 mm (screw terminals). Peck drill if your spindle is slow. Drilling from this side puts every hole in the middle of its bottom pad.
  5. Flip. Lift the board off and push a 3 mm pin (a dowel, or a 1/8 in drill bit shank down) into each alignment hole in the spoilboard. Leave them standing about 1 mm proud, less than the board thickness. Turn the board over left to right, so the top edge stays at the top, and press it down over the pins. Tape it down, then re-probe Z on the new surface. Keep the same X and Y zero.
  6. Side 2: copper. Load the …_top.dxf file. Engrave ISO_CENTER and pocket CLEAR_POCKET exactly as on side 1. Don't drill again. Check that the isolation rings are centred on the holes. If they are all off by the same amount, the zero has moved.
  7. Side 2: slot, then outline. First pocket the SLOT shape right through, about 1.7 mm deep for 1.5 mm stock, with an end mill of 1.5 mm or less (1/16 in is fine): it is 2 mm wide and runs out through the top edge beside U1, and it is on its own layer so a wide outline cutter never has to squeeze into it. Then cut the OUTLINE rectangle right through, on the outside of the line, with a 1.6–3 mm end mill. Leave tabs. If your blank is already 150 × 100, you can skip the outline, but always cut the slot.
  8. Deburr, scrub both sides with a scouring pad, and check with a meter that no two neighbouring traces touch on either side, and that no lead hole touches the top ground copper.
  9. Optional: a coat of flux or solder resist keeps the copper bright.

Why CLEAR_POCKET matters: the ISO_CENTER cut only makes a narrow groove around each copper shape. Copper that belongs to no shape, such as the 8 mm zone around the lathe side and the edge margin, would otherwise stay on the board as loose islands, and an island can bridge the lathe side to the ESP32 side across two thin grooves. Always pocket the CLEAR_POCKET areas, especially in the top right corner. The pocket edges touch the copper, so the pocket's first pass runs in the same groove the ISO cut made; that is intended, and harmless. If your CAM can clear everything that is not copper on its own (for example FlatCAM's non-copper clearing from the Gerbers), that does the same job.

The side 1 file is mirrored left to right because you look at the bottom from underneath: J1–J6 appear on the right and J7–J9 on the left. Flipping the board left to right around the alignment pins brings it back, so the side 2 file is not mirrored and the same zero works for both.

Do the pull-up resistors need to be there with short cables?

Yes, but not because of the cable length. The E6B2-CWZ6C has NPN open-collector outputs: its transistor can pull a line down to 0 V, but nothing inside the encoder ever drives the line high. Without a pull-up, a line that is not being pulled low just floats, and the ESP32 reads noise. The pull-up is what makes the "high" half of every pulse.

The ESP32 has its own internal pull-ups, about 45 kΩ. With 4–5 inch cables those would probably work. The board uses 2.2 kΩ anyway, for two reasons:

  • Noise. The encoders sit next to stepper motors and their drivers, which switch amps of current very fast. A 2.2 kΩ line is 20 times stiffer than 45 kΩ, so induced spikes don't become false counts.
  • Speed. A weak pull-up makes slow rising edges. At 600 P/R the X and Z lines can switch tens of thousands of times a second during a rapid, and slow edges cost counts.

Long cables make both problems worse, which is why you hear pull-ups talked about for long runs, but the open-collector output needs one at any length. The spindle lines have their pull-ups too: the G540 inputs pull them up, and R6, R8 and R10 pull up the inverted copies the ESP32 reads.

What is the PC817?

The PC817 is an optocoupler: an infrared LED and a light-sensitive transistor sealed in one small 4-pin chip, facing each other across an insulating gap. The ESP32 lights the LED; the light switches the transistor on. No current crosses the gap, so the lathe controller and the ESP32 are never electrically connected.

How it sets the speed: the ESP32 flashes the LED 2000 times a second, on for a fraction of each cycle. The transistor connects HIGH to WIPER for the same fraction, and the 4.7 kΩ and 10 µF smooth that into a steady voltage at WIPER, just as the pot's wiper would have. More on-time means a higher voltage and a faster spindle. If the ESP32 stops or restarts, the LED goes dark and the 4.7 kΩ pulls WIPER down to LOW, so the spindle slows to a stop.

Before you connect J9, measure the pot circuit. With the lathe powered and the pot still in place, measure DC and AC volts between HIGH and LOW, and between each of them and mains earth. HIGH to LOW must be under 25 V, the rating of C1 (it is usually 5–12 V). If either pin reads anything against earth, the whole pot circuit floats at mains potential, which the PC817 and the gap are designed for: still treat J9, R14, C1 and the lathe side of U1 as live, keep them inside the controller enclosure, and never let a lead, wire or tool bridge the gap. If HIGH to LOW is more than 25 V, or you cannot measure it, do not connect the board.

Why the G540 is still connected

The ESP32 does not need the G540, but UCCNC does. Threading with G33, including every program from the Thread Wizard, needs UCCNC to see the spindle encoder through the G540's inputs. So the spindle encoder's signals go to both: to the G540 for UCCNC, and through level shifter LS1 to the ESP32. The X and Z encoders go only to the ESP32. The step and direction pins are no longer tapped.

UCCNC settings

This project does not change UCCNC. If UCCNC already reads the spindle encoder, keep its input settings: the index on input 1 (port 1, pin 10), A on input 2 (pin 11) and B on input 3 (pin 12), as the G540 maps them. Check UCCNC's input diagnostics after wiring: those three inputs must still change as you turn the chuck by hand.

From the Geckodrive G540 manual Rev 8. Use stranded 14–24 AWG wire in the G540 terminals, and do not fit wire plugs.

  1. If the board can't join a network, it opens access point ESP32-Lathe (password lathe1234). Join it and go to http://192.168.4.1. The blue LED flashes very fast while the board is in this mode.

Calibrate each axis

  1. Mount a dial indicator against the slide, then zero both the indicator and the DRO.
  2. Move the slide 1.000 in by the indicator, always approaching from the same direction.
  3. Read the DRO. If it differs, set travel per encoder rev to old value × indicator ÷ DRO. For X shown as diameter, compare the DRO with twice the indicator reading.
  4. Repeat until the readings agree within a few counts. One count is travel per rev ÷ 2400, which is 0.00004 in for a 0.1 in lead.
